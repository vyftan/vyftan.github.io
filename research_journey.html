From Machine Learning to Information Theory and Back. Twice?

By Vincent Y. F. Tan

In July 2026, on the advice of a former student, I asked ChatGPT a question I had been thinking about for more than a decade. It concerned the Gaussian multiple-access channel (MAC): two senders transmit to one receiver, which observes the sum of their signals plus noise. The capacity region tells us which pairs of rates the two senders can achieve in the limit of long blocks. I wanted to know how close they could get with a block of finite length and a fixed probability of error. At the two corner points of the region, I had a good idea of the answer. What I had never managed to do was prove it.

Why that problem? And why, after spending much of the past eight years on bandits, was I thinking about it again? To explain that, I have to go back to 2005, my final undergraduate year at Cambridge.

I had my first taste of research at the Signal Processing Laboratory, where Dr. Cédric Févotte supervised my project on blind audio source separation. I was an engineering student with little experience writing proofs. My question was empirical: which transforms worked best for separating audio sources? I tried the discrete cosine transform, its modified version, and bases chosen from wavelet packets, among others. What I remember most vividly is running MATLAB on several machines in the lab at once and being told, quite reasonably, that other people might like to use them too.

At MIT, I joined Alan Willsky’s group and spent much of my first year looking for a thesis topic. A detour into signal processing led to my first journal paper, with Vivek Goyal, on denoising signals with a finite rate of innovation. Then Sujay Sanghavi, a postdoc in Alan’s group, introduced me to the Chow–Liu algorithm for learning tree-structured graphical models. The algorithm was elegant, but what caught my attention was a question about its errors: as we collect more samples, how quickly does the probability of learning the wrong tree fall? That question occupied most of my PhD and led to papers on discrete Markov trees and Gaussian trees that I remain proud of. With my labmates, I also worked on learning trees with latent variables and on learning the structure of Ising models, including those on Erdős–Rényi random graphs.

The summer of 2010 changed what I wanted from a research problem. Da Wang, John Sun, Hye Won Chung, and I got together at MIT to read the lecture notes that became Abbas El Gamal and Young-Han Kim’s book Network Information Theory. I was struck by the precision of the questions. Sometimes the goal was a single number: the highest rate at which a sender could communicate reliably. Sometimes it was a set: all the rate pairs at which two senders could communicate simultaneously. An achievability proof showed which rates were possible; a converse ruled out rates that were impossible. I found it beautiful when the two met.

Later, during my postdoc in Wisconsin, I read Amir Ingber and Yuval Kochman’s paper on lossy source coding dispersion. It asked how much extra rate you need to compress a source when you have only a finite block to work with. Here was a question I wanted to ask about the network problems I had come to love: once you know the limit, how quickly can you approach it?

I approached Oliver Kosut, then a postdoc in Alan’s lab. Our paper was my first in core information theory and the first of many on second-order asymptotics. My favourite result was our characterization of the second-order rate region for Slepian–Wolf coding. For the MAC and the broadcast channel, we obtained inner bounds. Those were the results I kept thinking about: we could show what rates were achievable, but could anyone do better?

I returned to Singapore in 2012 for a stint at A*STAR’s Institute for Infocomm Research. That October, at Allerton, I met Jonathan Scarlett, who was working on better inner bounds for the discrete memoryless MAC. The following year, as I queued to board my flight home from ISIT in Istanbul, it occurred to me that we might be able to prove a matching converse for the Gaussian MAC with degraded message sets, in which one sender knows both messages. I contacted Jonathan, and we got to work. The correlation between the two codewords mattered on both sides of the proof: in the converse we restricted attention to codeword pairs with nearly the same correlation; in the achievability argument we perturbed the correlation slightly. The bounds matched. It was the beginning of a long collaboration.

Around the same time, I began working with another future colleague, Marco Tomamichel. Marco came from quantum information theory, but we shared a taste for finding the next term in an asymptotic expansion, whether the problem was classical or quantum. One of my favourite results from that collaboration was a sharp third-order converse for a broad class of discrete memoryless channels. Those were probably my most productive research years before AI. Until recently, it would never have occurred to me to add that qualification.

Between 2014 and 2017, in my early years at NUS, Silas Fong and I proved strong converses for the Gaussian multiple-access channel and the Gaussian broadcast channel. From roughly 2018 to 2021, Lei Yu and I worked on several open problems in common information. That collaboration eventually led to a monograph. Looking back, I think the second-order papers did much to earn me tenure and promotion to associate professor. Tenure also brought a fairly blunt question: what next?

The information theory problems I most wanted to solve were still there. I had simply run out of ideas for solving them. And, to be candid, I didn’t think another several years of trying the same approaches would get me to full professor. Around that time, I came across work by Daniel Russo and Benjamin Van Roy on using information theory to bound Bayesian regret in multi-armed bandits. I suggested it to Zixin Zhong, who had just started her PhD with me. We didn’t get very far with that particular idea. Zixin found other bandit problems to work on, and so did I.

From about 2018 onward, my group and I spent much of our time on bandits. We had published mainly in IEEE journals and at IEEE conferences; now we were writing for machine learning conferences. I’m particularly proud of Junwen Yang’s work on best-arm identification in linear bandits and his asymptotically optimal algorithm for clustering with bandit feedback. I believe this body of work helped earn me promotion to full professor. And it was Junwen, of all people, who suggested that I try using AI on one of my old information theory problems.

I was initially sceptical about what ChatGPT or Claude could do for me. Still, in July and August of 2026, I gave Junwen’s suggestion a try. I chose the Gaussian MAC because I knew exactly where my previous attempts had failed. I could describe the capacity region, state the conjectured second-order region at the corner points, and explain why the converse had resisted me. I put all of that into ChatGPT, using Ultra mode on the advice of my former postdoc Recep Can Yavas. Over about two weeks of intermittent exchanges, I questioned individual steps and simplified arguments that were too long for my liking. By the end, I was convinced I had a proof. It took another fortnight to write the corner-point paper in my own words. After so many years, it felt good to send Jonathan, Recep, and a few others a converse rather than another inner bound.

By then I was serving as Deputy Head for Graduate Programmes in Mathematics and on the University Promotion and Tenure Committee. I hadn’t suddenly acquired more hours in the day. What had changed was how many ideas I could try in the hours I had. It reminded me of my first run in carbon-plated shoes: my watch still said 4:30 per kilometre, a brisk pace for me, but my legs were telling a different story.

Next I turned to the relative interior of the Gaussian MAC sum-rate face, expecting the main challenge to be another converse. The surprise came on the achievability side. The familiar strategy uses the same power split between the two senders at every channel use. That suffices to attain every point on the first-order sum-rate face. But at points strictly between the corners, when the target error probability is below one half, varying the split across channel uses reduces the second-order backoff from sum capacity. I had spent years asking how to prove that the existing inner bound was tight. Here it turned out that we could do better.

Then came the problem I had most wanted to understand: the Gaussian broadcast channel. Its capacity characterization involves an auxiliary random variable. My problem was to identify something in an arbitrary code that could play the same role in a second-order converse. Working with Astra led me to a connection I doubt I would have made on my own. The strong functional representation lemma of Cheuk Ting Li and Abbas El Gamal gave me a way to partition the stronger receiver’s message set. The label of the part containing the message could serve as the auxiliary. What delighted me was that the tool had been sitting within information theory all along. I just hadn’t known to look there.

What do I make of AI’s role in all this? Its most valuable contribution was suggesting a connection I could recognise once I saw it, but was unlikely to propose myself. It also gave me a reason to learn subjects I had known only in passing, among them Gaussian functional inequalities and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes. The two fortnights I spent on the corner-point paper taught me something else: it took about as long to turn the argument into a proof I was willing to sign as it did to find the argument in the first place. I still go through every line. If I can’t explain why an inequality holds or what a quantity is doing in the proof, that’s where I stop. Sometimes the line needs a better explanation. Sometimes it needs a different proof.

I still work on bandits and online learning with my group, so this is hardly a farewell to machine learning. But I am back to spending my spare research hours on information theory problems I couldn’t solve as a postdoc or a young faculty member. What I find especially satisfying is that those unsuccessful years turned out to be useful after all. When Junwen suggested trying AI, I knew exactly which question to ask first.

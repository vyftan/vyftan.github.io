From Machine Learning to Information Theory and Back. Twice?

By Vincent Y. F. Tan

In July 2026, on the advice of a former student, I put a problem to ChatGPT that I had been thinking about for more than a decade. It concerned the Gaussian multiple-access channel, a model simple enough to explain in a sentence: two senders transmit to one receiver, and the receiver observes the sum of their signals plus noise. I knew what the answer ought to be at the two corner points of its capacity region. What I had never managed to do was prove it.

Why did I choose that problem? And why, after spending much of the past eight years working on bandits, was I back to thinking about it at all? For that, I have to go back to Cambridge in 2005.

I had my first taste of research at the Signal Processing Laboratory, where Dr. Cédric Févotte supervised my project on blind audio source separation. I was an engineering student with little experience writing proofs. My question was empirical: which transforms worked best for separating audio sources? I tried Fourier, wavelet, discrete cosine, and adaptive transforms. What I remember most vividly is running MATLAB on several machines in the lab at once and being told, quite reasonably, that other people might like to use them too.

At MIT, I joined Alan Willsky’s group and spent much of my first year looking for a thesis topic. A detour into signal processing led to my first journal paper, with Vivek Goyal, on denoising signals with a finite rate of innovation. Then someone introduced me to the Chow–Liu algorithm for learning tree-structured graphical models. The algorithm was elegant, but what caught my attention was a question about its errors: how quickly does the probability of learning the wrong tree decay as we collect more samples? That question occupied most of my PhD and led to papers on discrete Markov trees and Gaussian trees that I remain proud of. I also worked with labmates on learning trees with latent variables and on learning Erdős–Rényi random graphs.

The summer of 2010 changed what I wanted from a research problem. Four of us at MIT got together to read the lecture notes that became Abbas El Gamal and Young-Han Kim’s book Network Information Theory. I was struck by the precision of the questions. Sometimes the goal was to find a single number: the largest rate at which one could communicate reliably. Sometimes it was to find a set: all the rate pairs at which two users could communicate simultaneously. An achievability proof put rates inside that set; a converse ruled out everything outside it. I found it beautiful when the two met. Later in my PhD, I read Amir Ingber and Yuval Kochman’s paper on lossy source coding dispersion. It asked how far one was from the fundamental limit when the blocklength was finite. I had found the question that would keep bringing me back to information theory.

I approached Oliver Kosut, then a postdoc in Alan’s lab. Our paper was my first in core information theory and the first of many on second-order asymptotics. The result I particularly liked was our characterization of the second-order rate region for Slepian–Wolf coding. For the multiple-access channel (MAC) and the broadcast channel, we obtained inner bounds. Those results bothered me in a productive way: we could show what rates were achievable, but not whether anyone could do better.

I returned to Singapore in 2012 for a stint at A*STAR’s Institute for Infocomm Research. That October, at Allerton, I met Jonathan Scarlett, who was working on better inner bounds for the discrete memoryless MAC. On the flight home from ISIT in Istanbul the following year, it occurred to me that we might be able to get a matching converse for the Gaussian MAC with degraded message sets, a model in which one sender knows both messages. I contacted Jonathan, and we got to work. The correlation between the two codewords turned out to be the key on both sides of the proof: we fixed it approximately in the converse and perturbed it slightly in the achievability argument. The bounds matched. It was the beginning of a long collaboration with a future colleague.

Another future colleague I began working with around this time was Marco Tomamichel. Marco came from quantum information theory, but we shared a taste for finding the next term in an asymptotic expansion, whether the problem was classical or quantum. One of my favourite results from that collaboration was a sharp third-order converse for a broad class of discrete memoryless channels. Those were probably my most productive research years before AI. The qualification “before AI” is one I would not have thought to add until recently.

In my early years at NUS, Silas Fong and I proved strong converses for the Gaussian multiple-access channel and the Gaussian broadcast channel. Later, Lei Yu and I worked on several open problems in common information, a collaboration that eventually led to a monograph. Looking back, I think the second-order papers did much to earn me tenure and promotion to associate professor. Tenure also brought a fairly blunt question: what next?

It wasn’t that information theory had run out of problems. The ones I most wanted to solve were still there; I had simply run out of ideas for solving them. And, to be candid, I did not think that another several years of trying the same approaches would get me to full professor. Around that time, I came across work by Daniel Russo and Benjamin Van Roy on using information theory to bound Bayesian regret in multi-armed bandits. I suggested that my PhD student Zixin Zhong look into it. We didn’t get very far with that particular idea. Zixin found other bandit problems to work on, and so did I.

From about 2018 onward, bandits occupied much of my research time. The group changed with me: where we had once published mainly in IEEE journals and conferences, we were now writing for machine learning conferences. Among the results I’m particularly proud of are Junwen Yang’s work on best-arm identification in linear bandits and his asymptotically optimal algorithm for clustering with bandit feedback. I think it was this body of work that carried me through to full professor. There is a nice irony in what happened next: it was Junwen who suggested that I try using AI to solve an information theory problem.

I was initially skeptical about what ChatGPT or Claude could do for me. Still, in July and August this year, I gave Junwen’s suggestion a try. I chose the Gaussian MAC because I knew exactly where my previous attempts had failed. The first-order capacity region was known, the candidate second-order region at the corner points was known, and I could explain why the converse was difficult. I put all of that into ChatGPT. Over about two weeks of intermittent exchanges, I questioned individual steps and simplified arguments that were far too long. By the end, I was convinced that I had a proof. It took another fortnight to write the corner-point paper in my own words. I sent it to Jonathan and a few others who had thought seriously about the problem. After so many years, it felt good to be able to send them a converse rather than another inner bound.

I was by then serving as Deputy Head for Graduate Programmes in Mathematics and on the University Promotion and Tenure Committee. I hadn’t suddenly acquired more hours in the day. What had changed was how much I could try in the hours I had. The closest analogy I can think of is the first time I ran in carbon-plated shoes: the watch still said 4:30 per kilometre, but the pace felt rather different.

Next I turned to the relative interior of the Gaussian MAC sum-rate face, expecting the main challenge to be another converse. The surprise came on the achievability side. The familiar strategy uses the same power split between the two senders at every channel use. That suffices to attain every point on the first-order sum-rate face. But when the target error probability is below one half, varying the split across channel uses lets the senders operate closer to sum capacity at finite blocklength. I had spent years asking how to prove that the existing inner bound was tight. Here, part of the answer was that it wasn’t.

Then came the problem I had most wanted to understand: the Gaussian broadcast channel. Its capacity characterization involves an auxiliary random variable. In a second-order converse, I needed to say what, in an arbitrary code, could play the role of that auxiliary. Working with Astra led me to a connection I doubt I would have made on my own. The strong functional representation lemma of Cheuk Ting Li and Abbas El Gamal gave a way to partition the stronger receiver’s message set. The label identifying the part that contains the message could then serve as the auxiliary. What delighted me was that the tool had been sitting within information theory all along. I just hadn’t known to look there.

What do I make of the role of AI in all this? Its most valuable contribution was suggesting a connection I could recognize once I saw it, but was unlikely to propose myself. It also gave me a reason to learn properly several things I had known only in passing, among them Gaussian functional inequalities and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes. But the two fortnights I spent on the first paper taught me something else. It took about as long to turn the argument into a proof I was willing to sign as it did to find the argument in the first place. I still go through every line. When I cannot explain why an inequality holds, or what a newly introduced quantity is doing in the proof, that is where the work resumes. Sometimes the line needs a better explanation. Sometimes it needs a different proof.

I still work on bandits and online learning with my group, so this is hardly a farewell to machine learning. But I am back to spending my spare research hours on the information theory problems I couldn’t solve as a young faculty member. What I find especially satisfying is that those unsuccessful years turned out to be useful after all. When Junwen suggested trying AI, I knew exactly which question to ask first.
